# LLM Agents and Tool Use: A Survey of Methods, Training Recipes, Benchmarks and Open Problems

## TL;DR
- Tool use evolved in two clear phases: prompting-only scaffolding (chain-of-thought [1], ReAct [2], PAL [3], Program-of-Thoughts [4]) and then training the behaviour into the model (Toolformer's self-supervised fine-tuning [5], Gorilla [6], ToolLLM [7]), converging on the vendored JSON-schema function-calling interface [8].
- Scaling from a handful of hand-coded tools to tens of thousands of APIs is a retrieval problem, not only a modelling problem: ToolLLM retrieves over 16,464 REST APIs [7] while the best retriever on the ToolRet benchmark reaches only nDCG@10 = 33.83 [9].
- The 2025 training frontier is reinforcement learning with tool feedback and outcome rewards - ToolRL reports +17% over base and +15% over SFT [10], ReTool reaches 67% on AIME in 400 training steps versus 40% for text-based RL at 1080 steps [11], and Search-R1 gains 24% over RAG baselines on Qwen2.5-7B [12].
- Every benchmark that measures an actual tool-using agent reports a large human-machine gap (GAIA 92% human vs 15% for GPT-4 with plugins [13]; WebArena 78.24% vs 14.41% [14]; OSWorld 72.36% vs 12.24% [15]), and independent audits argue the scores themselves are unstable, with LiveMCPBench aggregate scores moving 18.9 percentage points across reruns of the same setup [16].
- Safety and reliability are the acknowledged bottlenecks: tool poisoning achieves 72.8% attack success on o1-mini with under 3% refusal [17], and agentic task success collapses to near zero within sixteen steps for every model tested [18].

## Background
Tool use is the mechanism by which a language model stops being a closed-book text generator and starts acting: the model decides which external program to invoke, with which arguments, and how to fold the result back into its reasoning. A 2024 survey found the terminology itself unsettled, defining tools used by language models as "external programs" precisely because "many works adopt the term 'tool' in different ways" [19]. The prompting lineage starts with chain-of-thought, which defines a chain of thought as "a series of intermediate reasoning steps" and shows that prompting a 540B-parameter model with just eight exemplars reaches state-of-the-art accuracy on GSM8K, "surpassing even finetuned GPT-3 with a verifier" [1]. ReAct then observed that reasoning and acting "have primarily been studied as separate topics" and generated "both reasoning traces and task-specific actions in an interleaved manner", gaining an absolute success rate of 34% on ALFWorld and 10% on WebShop over imitation and reinforcement learning methods with only one or two in-context examples [2]. A large survey of tool learning with foundation models frames the same lineage as the shift from introspective reasoning, whose plans cannot adapt to intermediate results, to extrospective reasoning that "additionally considers feedback from the user and environment" [20].

In parallel, program-aided variants attacked chain-of-thought's arithmetic weakness: PAL noted models "often make logical and arithmetic mistakes in the solution part" and offloaded the solution step to a Python interpreter, beating PaLM-540b with chain-of-thought by "absolute 15% top-1" on GSM8K across 13 BIG-Bench Hard and other tasks [3]; Program of Thoughts reached "an average performance gain over CoT of around 12% across all datasets" by decoupling computation from reasoning [4]. Training then replaced prompting: Toolformer made "a model trained to decide which APIs to call, when to call them, what arguments to pass, and how to best incorporate the results" from nothing more than a handful of demonstrations per API, improving a 6.7B GPT-J model enough to outperform a much larger GPT-3 [5] - though it explicitly cannot chain tools because "API calls for each tool are generated independently" [5]. The industrial endpoint of this trajectory is function calling, where "a function is a specific kind of tool, defined by a JSON schema", declared in the request's `tools` parameter, and executed in five steps that leave execution to the application rather than the API [8].

## Theme 1: Learning to call tools - prompting, supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning
The field's methodological centre of gravity moved from prompt engineering to parameter updates, and the evidence for what each buys is now fairly detailed. Gorilla showed the supervised route directly: a finetuned LLaMA-based model "surpasses the performance of GPT-4 on writing API calls" and, combined with a document retriever, "demonstrates a strong capability to adapt to test-time document changes" while mitigating hallucinated API usage [6]. ToolLLM scaled the same idea to 16,464 real-world RESTful APIs across 49 categories from RapidAPI Hub and introduced a depth-first-search-based decision tree so models can "evaluate multiple reasoning traces and expand the search space"; the resulting ToolLLaMA "exhibits comparable performance to ChatGPT" and generalizes to unseen APIs [7]. Within that paper's own comparison, the planning algorithm matters more than the prompt format: pass rates on the average metric were 35.3 for ReAct, 44.5 for ReAct@N and 63.8 for the search-based DFSDT [7].

The 2025 shift was toward reinforcement learning with tool feedback. ToolRL presents "the first comprehensive study on reward design for tool selection and application tasks within the RL paradigm", argues that coarse-grained signals such as answer matching "fail to offer the finegrained feedback required for effective learning" when multiple tools are invoked with diverse parameters, and reports "a 17% improvement over base models and a 15% gain over SFT models" using GRPO [10]. Search-R1 lets the model "autonomously generate (multiple) search queries during step-by-step reasoning with real-time retrieval" using retrieved-token masking and a simple outcome-based reward, improving 24% (Qwen2.5-7B) and 20% (Qwen2.5-3B) over RAG baselines across seven QA datasets [12]. ReTool interleaves real-time code execution into natural-language reasoning and trains on task outcomes, reaching 67% on AIME with 400 training steps where text-based RL needed 1080 steps for 40%, and 72.5% in extended settings [11]. Strikingly, a systematic study across data, algorithm and reasoning mode found that "replacing stitched synthetic trajectories with real end-to-end tool-use trajectories yields a far stronger SFT initialization", that a "deliberative strategy with fewer tool calls outperforms frequent tool calls or verbose self-reasoning", and that with these recipes 4B-sized models can match 32B-sized ones [21]. The multi-turn frontier adds a simulated user to the loop: MUA-RL integrates "LLM-simulated users into the reinforcement learning loop", scoring 67.3 on TAU2 Retail, 45.4 on TAU2 Airline, 28.3 on TAU2 Telecom, 28.4 on BFCL-V3 Multi Turn and 82.5 on ACEBench Agent with a 32B model [22]. The remaining disagreement is about data provenance and reward shape rather than about whether RL works.

## Theme 2: Tool retrieval - making thousands of tools tractable
Once tool collections grow past the prompt budget, tool selection becomes an information-retrieval problem. ToolLLM addresses it with a neural API retriever (a Sentence-BERT dense retriever scoring embedding similarity between instruction and API document, chosen over BM25 and text-embedding-ada-002), and reports that feeding ToolLLaMA the top five retrieved APIs "even improves the performance (both pass rate and win rate) compared to the ground truth API set" - a useful result because "asking users to manually recommend APIs from a large pool may not be practical" with over 16,000 APIs [7]. Gorilla's document retriever plays the same role for test-time document changes [6], but the scale differs sharply: APIBench, which comprises HuggingFace, TorchHub and TensorHub APIs [6], is listed in ToolLLM's comparison table with only 3 tools and 0 real API calls, against ToolBench's 3,451 tools, 126,486 instances and 469,585 real API calls [7].

ToolkenGPT offers a third option that avoids both retrieval and full fine-tuning, representing "each tool as a token ('toolken')" with a learned embedding while keeping the base language model frozen, arguing that fine-tuning "can be both costly and restricted to a predefined set of tools" [23]. The bottleneck, however, is retrieval quality itself. ToolRet, a benchmark of 7.6k retrieval tasks over a corpus of 43k tools, reports that the best retriever reaches "an nDCG@10 of only 33.83", that all retrievers score "less than 35% in Completeness@10 and under 52% in recall@10", that re-ranking with MonoT5 decreases average nDCG@10 from 33.83 to 28.92, and that ColBERT "even underperform[s] compared to simple lexical-based matching approaches like BM25" [9]. The authors underline the stakes by noting that RapidAPI contains more than 52k tools and PyPI hosts over 600k frequently updated packages, and that training on the 200k-instance ToolRet-train set enhances downstream tool-use pass rates by 10%-20% [9]. This is the clearest evidence that the field's scaling story depends on retrieval systems that are currently weak.

## Theme 3: Code-as-action versus JSON function calling
The sharpest architectural dispute is whether an agent should emit a structured tool call or write executable code. CodeAct argues for consolidating actions "into a unified action space" of executable Python, criticising JSON/text action formats for a "constrained action space (e.g., the scope of pre-defined tools) and restricted flexibility (e.g., inability to compose multiple tools)", and reports that across 17 LLMs on API-Bank and a new benchmark CodeAct outperforms widely used alternatives by up to 20% higher success rate, with an instruction-tuning set of 7k multi-turn interactions and self-debugging agents [24]. Vendors have adopted the pattern for context economy: exposing MCP servers as code APIs rather than direct tool calls "reduces the token usage from 150,000 tokens to 2,000 tokens - a time and cost saving of 98.7%" because intermediate results "stay in the execution environment by default" [25]. The cost is explicitly acknowledged: "running agent-generated code requires a secure execution environment with appropriate sandboxing, resource limits, and monitoring" [25].

The comparative evidence is promising but not unanimous. A head-to-head assessment on BFCL v4 found that programmatic tool calling "matched or exceeded native JSON tool calling on 11 of 14 models", and on 13 of 14 under parallel fan-out, with the GPT-5.6 family posting a 10.6% improvement over the JSON baseline and the code path holding stable where the JSON baseline degraded 2.3% on average under context rot; but "three of fourteen models did not benefit" and the extension only applies to code-capable models [26]. Historically the same trade-off drove PAL and Program-of-Thoughts, which traded free-form reasoning for a single powerful interpreter tool and gained absolute 15% top-1 on GSM8K [3] and around 12% average over CoT [4]. The JSON path's counter-advantage is validation and portability: BFCL evaluates serial and parallel calls "using a novel Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) evaluation method that can easily scale to thousands of functions", including the "ability of models to abstain and reason in stateful multi-step agentic setting" [27]. In short, code actions buy composition and context efficiency at the cost of sandboxing infrastructure, while schema-validated tool calls buy determinism and easier evaluation.

## Theme 4: Multi-agent and role-divided tool use
A second family of designs distributes tool use across several agents instead of trying to make one controller reliable. AutoGen builds applications from "multiple agents that can converse with each other", where agents are "customizable, conversable, and can operate in various modes that employ combinations of LLMs, human inputs, and tools", with the rationale that cooperating agents "can help encourage divergent thinking, improve factuality and reasoning, and provide validation" [28]. Its documentation makes the "agent-as-tool" primitive concrete - "any `BaseChatAgent` can be used as a tool by wrapping it in a `AgentTool`" - and reveals a real engineering constraint of the pattern: because agents and teams "maintain internal state that would conflict with parallel execution", parallelism must be disabled, so multi-agent tool use serializes where single-agent tool use can fan out [29]. MetaGPT takes a different route, encoding "Standardized Operating Procedures (SOPs) into prompt sequences" and using "an assembly line paradigm to assign diverse roles", explicitly targeting the "logic inconsistencies due to cascading hallucinations caused by naively chaining LLMs" and claiming more coherent solutions on collaborative software engineering benchmarks [30].

The boundary between "multi-agent" and "tool-user in a conversation" is also blurring from the benchmark side: τ-bench emulates conversations between a language-model-simulated user and an agent given domain-specific API tools and policy guidelines [31], and MUA-RL's simulated users are trained against rather than merely evaluated by [22]. The trade-off across these designs is consistent: role division and user simulation buy robustness on long, stateful, policy-constrained tasks, at the cost of coordination overhead, serialized execution [29] and, in the LLM-as-user case, dependence on simulation fidelity.

## Theme 5: Benchmarks, leaderboards and evaluation validity
Tool-use evaluation matured from API-call correctness to agentic, stateful and multi-domain settings. API-Bank started small and runnable, with a "runnable evaluation system consisting of 73 API tools" and "314 tool-use dialogues with 753 API calls", training Lynx from Alpaca to surpass Alpaca's tool utilisation by more than 26 points and approach GPT-3.5 [32]. AgentBench broadened to "8 distinct environments" and over 29 models, concluding that "poor long-term reasoning, decision-making, and instruction following abilities are the main obstacles", and reporting the counter-intuitive negative result that "training on code present ambivalent impacts on different agent tasks" [33]. The human-machine gap then became the headline: GAIA's 466 real-world questions yield "92% vs. 15% for GPT-4 equipped with plugins" [13]; WebArena's best GPT-4-based agent reaches "an end-to-end task success rate of 14.41%, significantly lower than the human performance of 78.24%" [14]; and OSWorld's 369 computer tasks show humans at over 72.36% against 12.24% for the best model, "primarily struggling with GUI grounding and operational knowledge" [15].

The last two years added reliability and stability metrics rather than new capability records. τ-bench introduced "pass^k" to measure behavioural reliability and found that "even state-of-the-art function calling agents (like gpt-4o) succeed on <50% of the tasks, and are quite inconsistent (pass^8 < 25% in retail)" [31]; τ²-bench extended this to dual-control settings, modelled as a Dec-POMDP where "both agent and user make use of tools", and reports "significant performance drops when agents shift from no-user to dual-control" [34]. StableToolBench was built precisely because live APIs are unstable, proposing a virtual API server with caching and API simulators plus "solvable pass and win rates" so that "model performance assessed on them must be comparable over time" [35]. BFCL has iterated from "BFCL-v1 introducing AST as an evaluation metric" through enterprise and OSS-contributed functions and multi-turn interactions to "BFCL-v4 introducing holistic agentic evaluation", with Overall Accuracy defined as "the unweighted average of all the sub-categories" [36]; its own paper reports that "while state-of-the-art LLMs excel at singleturn calls, memory, dynamic decision-making, and long-horizon reasoning remain open challenges" [27]. MINT added the multi-turn/interaction dimension and two uncomfortable findings: "better single-turn performance does not guarantee better multi-turn performance", and "supervised instruction-finetuning (SIFT) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) generally hurt multi-turn capabilities" [37].

Independent evaluations now dispute how much any of these numbers mean. A validity audit of BFCL v4, τ²-Bench, LiveMCPBench and MCP-Atlas traced 496 expert-reviewed benchmark executions and found "92 evaluator-human disagreements, corresponding to an 18.5% misalignment rate", with deterministic evaluators failing through "brittle state matching, trajectory lock-in, annotation errors, substring checks, and reward-basis mismatch" and LLM judges through "rubric drift, hallucinated completion, answer-only scoring, and substantial run-to-run variance"; in LiveMCPBench, "repeated evaluation of the same setup changes the aggregate score by 18.9 percentage points", with aggregate scores from 57.9% to 76.8% across 23 valid runs (mean 69.4%, standard deviation 5.4 points) [16]. ToolFailBench adds that similar aggregate scores can hide different failure modes: across 1,000 tasks the best of 19 headline models reaches 86.33% Clean Tool-Use Rate, but Llama-3.1-70B "still calls tools on 77.73% of no-tool control tasks", and Llama-3.1-70B and Qwen2.5-72B differ by 89 percentage points on control-task accuracy [38]. The newest large-scale agentic benchmark, LiveMCPBench, uses 95 real-world tasks, 70 MCP servers and 527 tools with an LLM judge "achieving 81% agreement with human reviewers", where the best model (Claude-Sonnet-4) reaches a 78.95% success rate with large variance across models [39].

## Theme 6: Interoperability - the MCP ecosystem and its security surface
The most consequential recent change is not a model but an interface standard. Anthropic open-sourced the Model Context Protocol on 2024-11-25 as "a new standard for connecting AI assistants to the systems where data lives", with developers either exposing data through MCP servers or building clients that connect to them, shipping specification and SDKs, local server support in Claude Desktop and an open-source server repository with pre-built servers for Google Drive, Slack, GitHub, Git, Postgres and Puppeteer [40]. Adoption followed quickly: OpenAI's agent tooling released "the new Responses API" with built-in "web search, file search, and computer use" plus an Agents SDK built on Agents, "Handoffs", "Guardrails" and tracing, designed to work with other providers' Chat Completions-style endpoints [41], and the MCP specification's 2025-03-26 revision added "an authorization framework based on OAuth 2.1", replaced "HTTP+SSE transport with a more flexible Streamable HTTP transport", and added JSON-RPC batching and "comprehensive tool annotations ... whether it is read-only or destructive" [41]. Microsoft announced first-party support "across its agent platform and frameworks, spanning GitHub, Copilot Studio, Dynamics 365, Azure AI Foundry, Semantic Kernel and Windows 11" and joined the MCP Steering Committee [42]. On 2025-09-08 the protocol got a discovery layer: "the Model Context Protocol (MCP) Registry - an open catalog and API for publicly available MCP servers", with the registry and its OpenAPI specification open source, while the preview "does not provide data durability guarantees or other warranties" [42].

Scale measurements of this ecosystem vary but all run into the thousands: LiveMCPBench states that the number of MCP servers has surpassed 10,000 [39], while MCPCorpus collects "around 14K MCP servers and 300 MCP clients", each annotated with 20+ normalized attributes tracking identity, interface configuration and GitHub activity [43]. A dedicated MCP survey organises the analysis around a server lifecycle of "creation, operation, and update", assessing security and privacy risks per phase alongside industry adoption [44]. The security surface widens with each new server: Tool Poisoning embeds "malicious instructions ... within a tool's metadata without execution", and MCPTox - built on 45 live real-world servers, 353 tools and 1,312 malicious test cases across 10 risk categories - finds o1-mini reaching 72.8% attack success and several agents above 60%, with the highest refusal rate (Claude-3.7-Sonnet) under 3%, concluding that "existing safety alignment is ineffective against malicious actions that use legitimate tools for unauthorized operation" [17]. Design therefore matters: exposing servers as code APIs invites the agent to discover tools by exploring a filesystem [25], while the registry model centralises discoverability and therefore also the trust decision [42].

## Trends and open problems
Three shifts define the last two years. First, training replaced prompting: 2025 recipes consistently show RL with tool or outcome feedback beating both prompting and plain SFT - +17% over base and +15% over SFT [10], 24% over RAG for a 7B model [12], 67% versus 40% on AIME at a fifth of the training steps [11], and 4B models trained with the right data and reward shaping matching 32B models [21]. Second, interoperability consolidated around one protocol in under a year, from an Anthropic announcement [40] to OpenAI adoption [41], Microsoft first-party support [42] and an official registry [42], with a measurable ecosystem of thousands of servers [43][39] and a dedicated research literature on its landscape and risks [44]. Third, evaluation moved from "can it call the API" to "is it reliable, stateful and honest about not calling", which is where the discomforting results live [31][34][16][38].

Several problems remain genuinely open. Tool retrieval is the least resolved subsystem: the best retriever achieves nDCG@10 of 33.83 and ColBERT underperforms BM25 [9], even though retrieval is described as "a critical initial step" for large toolsets [9]. Whether scaling or better training fixes reliability is disputed rather than settled: long-horizon degradation follows "a geometric law governed by a single per-step reliability parameter, which rises with model scale but saturates well below 1 even for the strongest models", and on a real tool-use loop "every model tested, including widely deployed systems, falls from near-perfect success to near zero within sixteen steps", with degradation "driven by step count rather than context length" (logit slope -0.69 vs. -0.44, p=3x10-6) and tool-call format drift affecting 21% of trajectories [18]. A trustworthiness survey accordingly distinguishes techniques "supported by empirical evidence from those that remain largely conceptual", notes that "persistent memory can carry compromised information across sessions" and that "access to external tools can turn an incorrect model response into a consequential real-world action", and admits its own proposed six-phase Trustworthy Agent Development Lifecycle "has not yet been empirically validated" [45].

Security is the clearest unresolved area, and the disagreement is about mitigation philosophy rather than about the vulnerability. Indirect prompt injection is the founding result, injecting prompts into data likely to be retrieved so that "processing retrieved prompts can act as arbitrary code execution", with the authors stating that "effective mitigations of these emerging threats are currently lacking" [46]. OWASP's current guidance distinguishes direct from indirect injection, warns that "research shows that they [RAG and fine-tuning] do not fully mitigate prompt injection vulnerabilities", and has moved Excessive Agency up the risk list "given the increased use of agentic architectures", with root causes of "excessive functionality; excessive permissions; excessive autonomy" and controls such as minimising tools, avoiding open-ended tools like shell commands, requiring user approval and enforcing "complete mediation" in downstream systems rather than in the model [47]. An agent-security benchmark spanning 10 scenarios, over 400 tools and nearly 90,000 test cases reports "the highest average attack success rate of 84.30%" with only "limited effectiveness" from current defenses [48]. The practical counter-argument is capability restriction rather than filtering: the "lethal trifecta" of "access to your private data", "exposure to untrusted content" and external communication means an attacker "can easily trick it into accessing your private data and sending it to that attacker", and since "in security, 99% filtering is a failing grade", the recommendation is to "avoid that lethal trifecta combination entirely" - with MCP explicitly named as encouraging users "to mix and match tools from different sources" [49]. Sandboxing is the vendor's answer and has measurable cost side effects in both directions: enforcing "filesystem isolation" and "network isolation" prevents a prompt-injected agent from modifying system files or exfiltrating credentials, and "sandboxing safely reduces permission prompts by 84%" in internal usage, though the alternative of constant approval carries "approval fatigue" risk [50]. Evaluation validity, tool retrieval, horizon-dependent reliability and injection-proof permissioning are therefore the four problems this survey would put at the top of the agenda.
