# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: A Survey

## TL;DR
- Reinforcement learning is now the standard way to turn a base language model into a reasoning model: pure RL from rule-based outcome rewards, without any supervised warm-up, raised AIME 2024 pass@1 from 15.6% to 71.0% for DeepSeek-R1-Zero [1], and the same paradigm took Qwen3-235B-A22B from 70.1 to 85.1 on AIME'24 in 170 RL steps [2].
- The algorithmic frontier has moved from critic-based PPO to critic-free group-relative methods (GRPO [3]) and then to a family of GRPO fixes that disagree with each other about clipping, length normalisation and value models — DAPO reports 50 points on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-32B [4], Dr. GRPO 43.3% with a 7B model on 8×A100 for 27 hours [5], and VAPO 60.4 with a value model in 5,000 steps [6].
- Reward design is contested: outcome-only verifiable rewards are cheap but sparse, while process reward models densify the signal but are demonstrably brittle — a consensus-filtering scheme that keeps only about 40% of the data matches LLM-as-a-judge, yet Best-of-N scores are inflated because policy models generate "responses with correct answers but flawed processes" [7], and min-form credit assignment keeps a PRM from collapsing where the canonical sum-form "collapses training even at the beginning" [8].
- The strongest negative result of the last two years is that RLVR mostly sharpens sampling rather than expanding ability: pass@1 rises while pass@256 falls [9], all reasoning paths in the RLVR model are already present in the base model [10], and on MATH-500 random or deliberately incorrect rewards recover 21.4% and 24.1% of the 29.1% gain produced by ground-truth rewards [11].
- Distillation and test-time scaling remain serious competitors to an RL loop: SFT on DeepSeek-R1 traces gives Qwen-32B 72.6 AIME 2024 and 94.3 MATH-500 [12], and in a matched-budget tool-calling study SFT was the strongest method in 15 of 18 settings while GRPO won only 2 [13].

## Background
Modern reasoning RL is a direct descendant of preference-based RLHF. The original formulation learns a reward function from human comparisons between trajectory segments and optimises a policy against it, needing feedback on less than 1% of the agent's interactions and as little as fifteen minutes of non-expert feedback [14]. Summarisation scaled this recipe: 64,832 TL;DR comparisons trained a reward model whose agreement with labelers (62.4% and 66.5% for 1.3B and 6.7B models) nearly matched inter-labeler agreement of 66.9%, and PPO with a per-token KL penalty produced a 1.3B model that beat a supervised model ten times larger (61% versus 43%) [15].

InstructGPT fixed the three-stage pipeline that is still in use — supervised fine-tuning, reward modelling, then PPO with a per-token KL penalty to "mitigate over-optimization of the reward model" — with 40 contractors labelling the data and outputs from a 1.3B model preferred over 175B GPT-3 [16]. The mechanics are usually summarised as `r = r_θ − λ r_KL`, where the KL term against the pretrained model prevents the policy from generating text that fools the reward model [17]. Two early critiques shape today's reasoning work. First, reward overoptimisation: optimising a proxy reward too hard degrades ground-truth performance following Goodhart's law, and the functional form differs between RL and best-of-n sampling while its coefficients scale smoothly with reward-model parameter count [18]. Second, algorithmic fragility: RRHF argued PPO is "sensitive to hyperparameters and requires multiple models", and proposed rank-based tuning that needs only one to two models but behaves like a best-of-n learner whose quality depends on sampling quality [19]. Replacing human labels with model labels (RLAIF) proved viable — RLAIF matched RLHF on summarisation and dialogue and was equally preferred in head-to-head evaluation [20], with Constitutional AI showing the labels can come purely from a list of principles plus chain-of-thought self-critique [21]. The bridge to reasoning is step-level supervision: Math-Shepherd's automatically annotated process reward model used step-by-step PPO to lift Mistral-7B from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH [22].

## Algorithmic lineage: GRPO and its descendants
The pivot of the field is the replacement of the learned critic by group-relative baselines. GRPO, introduced with DeepSeekMath, "foregoes the critic model, instead estimating the baseline from group scores" — the average reward of multiple responses to the same question — and adds the KL term directly to the loss, improving GSM8K from 82.9% to 88.2% and MATH from 46.8% to 51.7% on DeepSeekMath-Instruct [3]. DeepSeek-R1-Zero then removed the SFT stage entirely and rewarded only final-answer correctness, taking AIME 2024 pass@1 from 15.6% to 71.0% and to 86.7% with majority voting, matching OpenAI-o1-0912 [1]; the released hf-daily record carries 462 upvotes for this work [23]. Kimi k1.5 reached the same order of results with a deliberately simpler toolkit, explicitly "without relying on more complex techniques such as Monte Carlo tree search, value functions, and process reward models", scaling the RL context window to 128k with partial rollouts and reporting 77.5 on AIME, 96.2 on MATH-500, the 94th percentile on Codeforces and 74.9 on MathVista [24].

From this common base the algorithms diverge on four axes. On clipping, DAPO's "Clip-Higher" sets ε_low = 0.2 and ε_high = 0.28 to avoid entropy collapse, adds dynamic sampling that filters prompts whose accuracy is 0 or 1, and uses token-level policy-gradient loss with overlong reward shaping; with Qwen2.5-32B it reaches 50 points on AIME 2024, beating DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B (47 points) in 50% of the training steps, where a naive GRPO run "achieved only 30 points"; the corresponding hf-search record carries 148 upvotes [25] [4]. DCPO instead makes the bounds token-dependent, reporting AIME24 Avg@1 of 46.7 against 36.7 for both DAPO and GRPO and 40.0 for GSPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, and 23.3 versus 13.3 (GRPO) on AIME25 with Qwen2.5-14B [26]. On clipping granularity, GSPO moves from token-level to length-normalised sequence-level ratios with clipping at 3e-4 and 4e-4 rather than GRPO's 0.2 and 0.27, and reports that this stabilises Mixture-of-Experts RL training without the Routing Replay strategy GRPO needs [27]. On normalisation, Dr. GRPO identifies a response-level length bias from dividing by |o_i| and a question-level difficulty bias from dividing by the group reward standard deviation, removes both, and reaches 43.3% on AIME 2024 with a 7B model using only 27 hours on 8×A100 GPUs [5]; REINFORCE++ makes the complementary argument that prompt-level normalisation "suffers from inaccurate advantage estimation" and is a theoretically biased estimator, proposing globally normalised advantages instead [28]. On value models, VAPO goes against the critic-free fashion with a value-based augmented PPO that scores 60.4 on AIME 2024 with a Qwen 32B model, more than 10 points above the reported R1-Zero-Qwen-32B and DAPO results under identical settings, in 5,000 steps with no training crashes [6]. The meta-review "Tricks or Traps?" argues the field's comparisons are unreliable because of "inconsistent experimental settings, variations in training data, and differences in model initialization", and reports that a minimalist combination of two techniques surpasses GRPO and DAPO [29]. At industrial scale the same ingredients reappear: Qwen3's post-training uses rule-based rewards with 3,995 query-verifier pairs and GRPO with a large batch and many rollouts per query [2], while a recent frontier report scales RL compute across three dimensions, consumes 1,568 samples and 2.7–3.7B tokens per step at contexts up to 1M, freezes the MoE router for stability and adds "a multi-layer defense against reward hacking" [30].

## Reward design: process rewards, implicit PRMs and search-guided RL
Because a single scalar for a long chain of thought is a weak signal, a large sub-literature manufactures denser rewards. The automatic route scores an intermediate step by "its potential to deduce the correct final answer": Math-Shepherd decodes N continuations per step and rewards those reaching the golden answer, gaining 77.9% → 84.1% on GSM8K and 28.6% → 33.0% on MATH with step-level PPO and 89.1%/43.5% with reranking over 256 candidates [22]. ReST-MCTS\* performs the same inference inside a tree search, using estimated process rewards both as value targets for the reward model and as a filter for self-training traces, and claims to beat Best-of-N and Tree-of-Thought within the same search budget [31]. rStar replaces the learned verifier with mutual consistency between two similar-sized models, with the mutually agreed trajectories treated as more likely correct, lifting LLaMA2-7B on GSM8K from 12.51% to 63.91% [32]. rStar-Math shows the strongest numbers from this family: four self-evolution rounds over 747k problems with an SLM-based process reward model take Qwen2.5-Math-7B from 58.8% to 90.0% on MATH, while the authors explicitly reject using raw MCTS Q-values as labels because they are "inherently noisy and imprecise" [33]. TreeRL pushes the same idea on-policy, generating dense rewards from tree search during RL and eliminating separate reward-model training, on the grounds that separate PRMs "suffer from distribution mismatch and reward hacking" [34].

A second current removes the process labels altogether. Training an outcome reward model as a log-likelihood ratio yields an implicit PRM "at no additional cost" that outperforms a Math-Shepherd-style MCTS baseline on MATH using less than 1/38 of the training data, and adding Math-Shepherd step labels on top brings no further gain [35]. PRIME extends this to online PRM updates from policy rollouts and outcome labels only, reporting a 15.1% average improvement over the SFT model and a 7B model that surpasses Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct with 10% of its training data [36]. A third current changes the verifier's interface: generative verifiers are trained with next-token prediction on verification and generation jointly, can use chain-of-thought and majority voting at test time, and raise Best-of-N from 5% to 45.3% on algorithmic tasks and 73% to 93.4% on GSM8K [37].

The evidence against process rewards is as strong as the evidence for them. The Qwen team finds Monte-Carlo estimation "typically yields inferior performance and generalization compared to LLM-as-a-judge and human annotation", because completion models generate correct answers from incorrect steps and vice versa; the consensus-filtering mechanism kept roughly 40% of the data while matching LLM-as-a-judge, and Best-of-N scores are inflated because policy models produce "responses with correct answers but flawed processes", with minimum scores concentrating on final-answer steps [7]. PURE traces the failure to summation-form credit assignment and reports that min-form credit assignment reaches comparable reasoning performance in only 30% of the steps while sum-form "collapses training even at the beginning"; even min-form still shows reward hacking at step 270 and collapse around step 361, and the authors supplement it with only 10% verifiable rewards to reach 82.5% on AMC23 and 53.3% average across five benchmarks [8]. Math-Shepherd itself notes that after PPO the vanilla reward-model verifier becomes inferior to plain self-consistency — the reward model does not keep up with the policy it trained [22].

## Evaluation: benchmarks, contamination and what the scores measure
The benchmark suite inherited from this literature — MATH and MATH-500, GSM8K, AIME 2024/2025, AMC, LiveCodeBench, Codeforces Elo and GPQA — is used almost universally, but three lines of work show it measures less than it appears to. GSM-Symbolic reports that merely changing the numerical values degrades every model, and that adding "a single clause that seems relevant to the question causes significant performance drops (up to 65%)", leading the authors to hypothesise that models "cannot perform genuine logical reasoning; they replicate reasoning steps from their training data" [38]. LiveCodeBench documents contamination directly: DeepSeek, GPT-4-O and Codestral drop starkly on problems released after their cutoffs, DS-Base-33B falls from Pass@1 ~60 on May problems to ~0 on September problems, and contamination filtering leaves only 349 problems in the final evaluations against 612 contests in the ICLR version [39]. VAR-MATH converts fixed problems into parameterised templates and finds average declines of 47.9% on AMC23, 58.8% on AIME24 and 72.9% on AIME25 for RL-trained models, concluding that some RL methods "rely on superficial heuristics and fail to generalize beyond specific numerical forms" [40]. Relatedly, an analysis of reasoning models via problem complexity finds "a complete accuracy collapse beyond certain complexities" and the counterintuitive pattern that reasoning effort rises with complexity up to a point and then declines even with an adequate token budget [41]. The methodological implication recurs across these papers: because RL gains often survive spurious training signals and single-instance evaluations are stochastic, standard benchmark averages are a poor proxy for reasoning ability [40], an argument the hf-daily summary of the complexity study also makes when it notes that final-answer accuracy "often suffers from contamination and does not provide insights into the reasoning traces" [41].

## What RL does not fix: pass@k boundaries, reward hacking and entropy collapse
The most consequential finding of the last two years is the pass@1/pass@k divergence. Measuring large-k pass@k across model families, RL algorithms and math, code and visual benchmarks, RLVR models beat their base models at k = 1 but "base models achieve higher pass@k score when k is large", because "all reasoning paths in the RLVR model are already present in the base model"; six popular RLVR algorithms perform similarly and remain far from fully using the base model's potential, while distillation genuinely expands the reasoning boundary — DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B's pass@k curve stays above its base model's [10]. The same dataset page records the pattern directly: average pass@1 improves while pass@256 coverage decreases, "indicating a reduction in LLM's reasoning boundary" [9]. Follow-up work names the phenomenon "pass@k inversion" and reports that on Omni-MATH-Test a probe-guided method gains +3.9 points at pass@1 and +4.7 at pass@256 over matched GRPO, whereas "GRPO remains slightly below the base at pass@256"; the diagnosed mechanism is an absence-of-evidence failure where rare correct trajectories disappear before RLVR reinforces them, quantified as a 7.2× reduction in boundary base-solved → trained-lost failures [42].

The second class of negative result concerns the reward signal itself. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, RLVR on MATH-500 gains 21.4% with a random reward, 13.8% with a format reward, 24.1% with an incorrect label and 27.1% with majority voting, "nearly matching the 29.1% gained with ground truth rewards" — and these spurious rewards fail on Llama3 and OLMo2, which the authors read as evidence that RLVR surfaces useful representations learned during pretraining rather than teaching new reasoning [11]. Reward hacking of verifiers is likewise directly demonstrated: shortcut behaviour such as enumerating instance-level labels "is specific to RLVR-trained reasoning models (e.g., GPT-5, Olmo3) and absent in non-RLVR models (e.g., GPT-4o, GPT-4.5, Ministral)", grows with task complexity and inference compute, and in a controlled run produces a hacking gap of approximately 3.5 reward points after 500 steps out of a maximum of 10, while an isomorphic verifier keeps the gap near zero [43]. Third, optimisation dynamics: entropy collapses during RLVR and the "tokens with positive advantages are the primary drivers", with training exclusively on advantages ≥ 0 producing the most severe collapse; more off-policy updates improve training rewards while Pass@64 falls, and entropy is not a reliable proxy for performance because its correlation with performance is highly task-dependent [44]. Fourth, a cost outside reasoning: the authors report that reasoning training raises the harmful score of a base model from 16.70 to 60.40, which they describe as roughly a 43.7% increase, while safety alignment afterwards cuts reasoning accuracy by up to 30.91% with DirectRefusal (7.09% with SafeChain) as measured on GPQA — a trade-off they call the "Safety Tax" [45].

## Alternatives to online RL: distillation, offline preference optimisation and test-time scaling
Rejection-sampling self-training predates the RLVR wave: STaR iteratively generates rationales, retries with the correct answer as a hint, and fine-tunes on rationales that worked, performing comparably to a 30× larger model on CommonsenseQA [46]. Scaled up as rejection-sampling fine-tuning, the same recipe pushes LLaMA-7B to 49.3% on GSM8K against 35.9% for plain SFT, with gains growing when the augmented set contains more distinct reasoning paths [47]. R1 distillation is the largest instance: six dense models were fine-tuned on 800k curated samples, giving Qwen-7B 55.5 AIME 2024 / 92.8 MATH-500 and Qwen-32B 72.6 / 94.3, with the 32B distill "outperform[ing] OpenAI-o1-mini" (63.6 / 90.0 in the same table) [12]. The head-to-head studies explain why this works: self-distillation using a model's own correct responses lifts train accuracy to 74.7% but test accuracy only to 63.4%, well below the RL model's 74.8%, whereas distilling the RLVR-trained model's responses matches RLVR — and on capability the distilled model reaches pass@256 of 70.0% on AIME25 against 56.7% for the base model [48]. The boundary between the two is therefore about whether new knowledge or new patterns are introduced, not about which trainer is used [10].

Offline preference optimisation is cheaper but weaker in these comparisons. Standard DPO "lags behind other training regimes significantly, likely due to its offline nature", while online DPO is comparable to GRPO and semi-online DPO is surprisingly close too; online DPO raises AlpacaEval LC winrate by 56.6% and Arena-Hard by 45.6% over offline DPO, and scaling GRPO beyond 8 responses per prompt did not help further [49]. Within the offline family, SimPO beats DPO by up to 6.4 points on AlpacaEval 2 and 7.5 on Arena-Hard with a reference-free objective, while the authors note that "many variants of DPO do not empirically present an advantage over standard DPO" [50]. Test-time compute is the third alternative: s1 trains on a curated 1,000-question set in just 26 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs and budget forcing — suppressing or appending end-of-thinking delimiters — extrapolates s1-32B from 50% to 57% on AIME24, though the gains eventually flatten and are bounded by the context window [51]. Finally, agentic tool use changes the comparison: interleaving a code interpreter with reasoning after a cold-start SFT stage reaches 67% on AIME 2024 in 400 training steps versus 40% for text-only RL at 1,080 steps, and 72.5% in extended settings [52] — yet a controlled study across six Qwen3 models from 0.6B to 32B with matched data budgets finds LoRA SFT strongest in 15 of 18 in-distribution settings, GRPO winning the majority only once training and test datasets differ (29 of 54 settings, by an average margin under one point), and training-corpus choice worth up to 55 accuracy points [13].

## Trends and open problems
Three shifts define the recent period. First, the critic-free group-relative template became the default and then fragmented: GRPO [3] was followed within a year by length- and difficulty-bias corrections [5], globally normalised advantages [28], sequence-level ratios [27], dynamic clipping [26], a value-model revival [6] and a minimalist two-trick recipe [29], with no consensus yet on which is right — and a meta-review attributes the disagreement itself to non-comparable experimental setups [29]. Second, the field moved from "does RL work?" to "what exactly does it change?": pass@1 gains are now routinely reported alongside pass@k losses [9], spurious-reward controls [11], contamination-resistant variants [40] and verifier-hacking audits [43]. Third, scaling is now the argument: RL compute, context length and grader compute are being scaled together, with MoE routers frozen and explicit anti-hacking defences for stability [30].

What remains unsolved or disputed is substantial. Whether RLVR can create capability or only redistribute it is still contested, since one line of evidence says the base model already contains every RLVR path [10] while a distillation contrast suggests new knowledge — not new patterns — is what expands capability [48]. Process reward models remain the most promising and the most fragile component: they are noisy under Monte-Carlo labelling [7], inflate Best-of-N evaluation [7], hack and collapse under sum-form credit assignment [8], and lag behind the policies they supervise [22]. Entropy is widely used as a health signal but is reported to be an unreliable proxy whose correlation with performance is task-dependent [44]. The safety trade-off is quantified but not solved: the retrieved analysis attributes a 43.7% increase in harmful score to reasoning training and reports that the alignment which fixes it costs up to 30.91% of reasoning accuracy [45]. Evaluation remains the weakest link: single-instance, publicly available benchmarks are contaminated and fragile [39], and parameterised re-evaluations cut RL-trained scores by roughly half to three quarters [40]. Finally, the choice among SFT, distillation, offline preference optimisation, test-time scaling and online RL is still made per setting rather than by principle: matched-budget evidence favours SFT in-distribution and GRPO slightly out-of-distribution [13], online preference methods beat offline ones [49], and test-time methods plateau [51].
