# Video and Multimodal Generation: A Survey of Architectures, Long-Video Systems, Evaluation and Open Problems

## TL;DR
- Modern video generation is a three-step lineage in images that was transplanted to video: denoising diffusion [1], then diffusion in a pretrained autoencoder's latent space [2], then the replacement of the U-Net by a transformer over latent patches [3]; current video systems still optimise exactly that VAE-latent-plus-DiT stack [4].
- The 2024-2026 open-weight wave consolidated on diffusion transformer plus flow matching: HunyuanVideo 1.5 at 8.3B parameters for consumer GPUs [5], Wan in 1.3B and 14B variants with 8.19 GB VRAM for the small one [6], and Open-Sora 2.0 trained for only $200k [7], while large models still need about 10 minutes for a 28-step video on a single H100 [8].
- The frontier has moved from single-clip quality to long, streaming video: frame-level autoregressive generation [9], sub-second latency at up to 29 FPS [10], infinite-length generation [11], and length extrapolation to 4 minutes and 15 seconds [12].
- Competing multimodal designs diverge sharply: shared-discrete-token autoregressive any-to-any models [13][14], masked discrete diffusion over the same token space [15][16], discrete flow matching [17], hybrid next-token-plus-diffusion transformers [18][19], and multimodal diffusion transformers [20].
- Evaluation is the weakest link: FVD barely reacts to temporal corruption [21], CLIP-based alignment correlates near zero with human judgement of temporal transitions [22], and the best text-to-video models satisfy caption and physics together in only 39.6% of cases [23] while all models stay below 0.60 on physical-law compliance [24].

## Background
Video generation is the task of synthesising a temporally coherent sequence of frames, usually conditioned on text or a first image; "multimodal generation" widens the same problem to models that emit or consume several modalities (text, image, audio, video) inside one system. The field matters now because open-weight models have closed most of the gap to proprietary ones in a single year [25][6][7], because the product question has shifted from clip quality to latency, memory and cost [26], and because transparency regulation of generated video is becoming legally binding [27].

The quantitative baseline came from images first. Denoising diffusion probabilistic models reported an Inception score of 9.46 and a state-of-the-art FID score of 3.17 on unconditional CIFAR10, with 256x256 LSUN sample quality similar to ProgressiveGAN, and framed sampling as a progressive lossy decompression scheme generalising autoregressive decoding [1]. For video, the Fréchet Video Distance and the StarCraft 2 Videos benchmark were introduced on top of the FID principles, using I3D features trained on Kinetics-400/600, and were argued to be the superior choice because SSIM and PSNR "can only account for the quality of individual frames" [28]. In parallel, the token-based route set its recipe with VideoGPT, which learned downsampled discrete latents with 3D convolutions and axial self-attention and then modelled them autoregressively with a GPT-like transformer using spatio-temporal position encodings [29].

Latent diffusion then defined the architecture that the rest of the field inherited: applying diffusion "in the latent space of powerful pretrained autoencoders" to cut the hundreds of GPU days of pixel-space training, with cross-attention layers turning the model into a flexible generator for text or bounding-box conditioning [2]. Extending this to video was done in two ways. Video Diffusion Models used a 3D U-Net, trained jointly on image and video data to reduce minibatch-gradient variance, and introduced conditional sampling for spatial and temporal video extension [30]; meanwhile latent-based systems such as Video LDM added temporal attention layers to a pretrained text-to-image model and MagicVideo introduced a low-dimensional latent embedding via a pretrained VAE, with pixel-based alternatives represented by Make-A-Video, Imagen Video and PYoCo [31]. The final piece was the Diffusion Transformer, which replaced the U-Net backbone with a transformer operating on latent patches and reached a state-of-the-art FID of 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256x256, with FID falling consistently as Gflops rose [3]. Surveys now trace this exact chain forward, listing VQ-VAE/VQ-GAN to VideoGPT and MAGVIT, DDPM/LDM to Stable Video Diffusion and VideoCrafter2, and DiT to Wan and HunyuanVideo [32].

## Foundations: latent space, transformers and the two surviving paradigms
The foundational literature is best read as a settlement of three design questions: where to denoise, what backbone to use, and what to model.

First, denoising in latent space rather than pixel space became the default because it is dramatically cheaper, but it introduces a compression bottleneck that is still being negotiated. Latent diffusion established the recipe and its cross-attention conditioning [2]; the first video diffusion model instead worked in pixel space with a 3D U-Net [30], and surveys explicitly split the next generation into "pixel-based VDMs" (Make-A-Video, Imagen Video, PYoCo) and "latent-based VDMs" (Video LDM, MagicVideo), noting that Show-1 integrates both strengths to produce high-resolution videos at a low computational cost of 15G GPU memory during inference [31]. That trade-off has not disappeared: the survey warns that "it is challenging for such a small latent space ... to cover rich yet necessary visual semantic details as described by the textual prompt" [31], and 2026 work on generation-aware latent compression still reports that a higher compression ratio degrades reconstruction quality, requires more channels, slows DiT convergence, and forces the pretrained DiT to be retrained or expensively adapted [4]. In other words, the VAE is not a preprocessing detail but a first-class design variable.

Second, the backbone converged on the transformer. DiT showed monotone gains with increased depth, width or token count and beat all prior diffusion models on ImageNet 512x512 and 256x256 [3], and the video translation of that idea — latent diffusion transformers that extract spatio-temporal tokens and model the video distribution in latent space — followed shortly after [31]. What remained genuinely contested was the temporal modelling choice inside that backbone: the pixar-era option was a 3D U-Net trained jointly on images and video [30], while the modern option is full spatio-temporal attention inside a diffusion transformer [6].

Third, the autoregressive alternative never went away. The VQ-VAE-plus-transformer recipe of VideoGPT was competitive with state-of-the-art GANs on the BAIR Robot dataset and generated high-fidelity natural video from UCF-101 and TGIF [29]. Surveys describe the modern version of this paradigm as a two-stage pipeline in which a VQ-VAE first compresses frames into discrete tokens and a transformer is then trained autoregressively on them, with the tokenizer itself evolving through MAGVIT's 3D-VQ autoencoder, MAGVIT-v2's replacement of codebooks by Lookup-Free Quantization, and VidTok's Finite Scalar Quantization [32]. The three-paradigm framing — GAN-based, diffusion-based and autoregressive — is now the standard way of organising the field's history, even though the published frontier has overwhelmingly become diffusion-transformer based [32][6].

## The 2024-2026 open-weight wave: flow matching, scale and long video
The most important change of the last two years is that the open-weight ecosystem stopped lagging. HunyuanVideo was explicitly positioned against the fact that leading models "remain closed-source, resulting in a notable performance gap between industry capabilities and those available to the public", and claimed performance comparable to or surpassing leading closed-source models [25]. Its successor then turned the claim into a scale-efficiency trade-off: 8.3 billion parameters achieving state-of-the-art visual quality and motion coherence with inference on consumer-grade GPUs, via an advanced DiT architecture with selective and sliding tile attention, glyph-aware bilingual text encoding, progressive pre- and post-training, and a video super-resolution network [5]. Wan makes the same trade-off explicit by shipping two sizes, 1.3B for efficiency and 14B for effectiveness, built on the diffusion transformer paradigm combined with flow matching, a spatio-temporal VAE and full spatio-temporal attention; the 14B model was trained on billions of images and videos to demonstrate scaling laws with respect to both data and model size, the 1.3B model needs only 8.19 GB of VRAM, and the report claims the first model able to generate visual text in both Chinese and English across up to eight tasks [6]. On its own Wan-Bench weighting (14 metrics over dynamic quality, image quality and instruction following) Wan 14B scores 0.724 and Wan 1.3B 0.689, against Sora at 0.700 and Hunyuan at 0.673 — a useful illustration of how narrow the gaps between systems have become [6]. Open-Sora 2.0 attacks the remaining barrier from the cost side, presenting a commercial-level model trained for only $200k and arguing that the cost of training a top-performing video generation model is highly controllable through data curation, architecture and training strategy [7].

The second change is that inference cost, not training cost, became the binding constraint. Open-source video diffusion models have scaled past 10 billion parameters, and MagicDistillation states the resulting price concretely: roughly 10 minutes to synthesise a 28-step video on a single H100, with additional weakness in portrait video synthesis for image-to-video variants [8]. Three complementary answers are visible in the record. The first is architectural: frame-level autoregressive frameworks such as LongLive combine causal attention, KV-recache, streaming long tuning and short window attention to make long video interactive in real time [9]. The second is distillation: MotionStream distils a motion-controlled text-to-video model into a causal student using Self Forcing with Distribution Matching Distillation and sliding-window causal attention with attention sinks, reaching sub-second latency and up to 29 FPS [10]. The third leaves the model alone and sparsifies attention, with MC-Sparse diagnosing sparse-attention quality loss as arising from token grouping constraints, inaccurate interaction selection and discarded attention contributions, and then recovering 1.80x denoising speedup on Minimax-H3-Base and 2.32x on 3D asset generation at negligible quality loss in a training-free framework [33].

The third change is that "long video" became a research object with its own machinery, and the reported gains are large but structurally restricted. Stable Video Infinity targets infinite-length generation with high temporal consistency by fine-tuning the diffusion transformer with error recycling [11]. Self-Forcing++ states the underlying problem precisely — teacher models cannot synthesise long videos, so students extrapolate beyond their training horizon and compound errors in continuous latent space — and answers it by sampling segments from self-generated long videos without long-video supervision, reporting extension up to 20x beyond the teacher's capability, more than 50x longer than its baseline, and videos up to 4 minutes and 15 seconds, equal to 99.9% of the maximum span its base model's position embedding supports [12]. A related but distinct strategy is to enforce the temporal guarantee structurally rather than statistically: OuroWorld turns static 3D Gaussian Splatting scenes into looping cinemagraphs, using a Fourier-series deformation field that guarantees looping by construction and a Grounded Drift Field anchored at the reference view to absorb cross-view inconsistency, and reports winning 70.8%-99.0% of user-study comparisons across 39 reconstructed and generated scenes under a ground-truth-free evaluation [34].

## Competing designs: unified any-to-any models, hybrid AR-diffusion and MMDiT
Away from video-only pipelines, the retrieved literature shows four competing families that differ mainly in what they share across modalities: a token vocabulary, a loss, a backbone, or nothing at all.

The first family unifies modalities in a shared discrete vocabulary trained with next-token prediction. MIO is presented as the first open-source any-to-any foundation model spanning text, image, speech with voice, and video, with SEED-Tokenizer quantising a 224x224 image into 32 tokens from a codebook of size 8192, SpeechTokenizer encoding speech at 50 tokens per second per codebook and 400 tokens per second across eight codebooks reduced to a 1024 x 4 = 4096 speech vocabulary, and the LLM embedding layer extended by 4096 + 8192 + 4 = 12292 tokens — which is what enables interleaved video-text generation [14]. AR-Omni pushes the same idea to a single ~7B-parameter Transformer decoder with no expert decoders, sharing the joint vocabulary V = V_text ∪ V_speech ∪ V_image and reaching a 0.88 real-time factor for streamed speech, while conceding that diffusion-free autoregressive image generation still lags behind diffusion-based systems [13].

The second family keeps the shared discrete token space but replaces serial decoding with masked discrete diffusion. Omni-Diffusion is described as the first any-to-any multimodal language model based on mask-based discrete diffusion, unifying text, speech and image with a unified probabilistic formulation over multimodal discrete tokens and an entropy-based decoding strategy at inference [15]. Dynin-Omni extends the masked-diffusion formulation to an omnimodal foundation model covering text, image and speech understanding and generation plus video understanding, explicitly contrasting itself both with autoregressive unified models that serialise heterogeneous modalities and with compositional unified models that orchestrate external modality-specific decoders, and reports 87.6 on GSM8K, 1733.6 on MME-P, 61.4 on VideoMME, 0.87 on GenEval and 2.1 WER on LibriSpeech test-clean across 19 multimodal benchmarks [16]. A third variant uses neither: NExT-OMNI is built on discrete flow matching for any-to-any omnimodal understanding and generation [17].

The third family avoids discretisation of images entirely and fuses two losses in one transformer. Transfusion combines the language modelling loss (next-token prediction) with diffusion to train one transformer over mixed-modality sequences, pretraining models up to 7B parameters and 2T multi-modal tokens with causal attention for text and bidirectional attention within each image, compressing each image to just 16 patches, and reporting that it scales significantly better than quantising images and training a language model over discrete image tokens — a direct empirical challenge to the first two families [18]. Show-o occupies the same hybrid slot but bolts discrete diffusion onto a pretrained LLM with only a QK-Norm addition, expanding the embedding layer by 8,192 new learnable embeddings for discrete image tokens and requiring approximately 20 times fewer sampling steps than autoregressive image generation; on its reported table a 1.3B Show-o reaches GenEval 0.68 versus 0.63 for a 7.3B Transfusion and 0.39 for a 7B Chameleon [19]. The disagreement between these two papers about tokenisation versus continuous diffusion is one of the clearest unresolved disputes in the multimodal generation literature.

The fourth family is the multimodal diffusion transformer, which changes conditioning rather than tokenisation. MM-DiT is described as the dominant approach in state-of-the-art models such as Stable Diffusion 3 and Flux.1 because it concatenates the input projections of both modalities and performs a single full attention operation, allowing bidirectional information flow between text and image branches, in contrast to the unidirectional cross-attention of U-Net architectures; the retrieved analysis counts 24 and 38 dual-branch blocks in SD3 and SD3.5-Large respectively, and 19 dual-branch followed by 38 single-branch blocks in the 12B-parameter Flux.1, with the single-branch variant cutting block parameters from 340M to 141M [20]. The contrast with specialised video pipelines is therefore structural: video-only diffusion keeps a dedicated denoising transformer with separate text cross-attention and no discrete vocabulary [6], whereas unified models trade image fidelity for one backbone, one loss or one token space — exactly the trade-off AR-Omni admits when it notes that autoregressive image generation still trails diffusion-based systems [13].

## Evaluation: multi-dimension suites, human protocols and negative results
Evaluation of generative video has converged on multi-dimension suites, but the quantitative evidence about what those suites measure is largely negative, and that is the most important finding in this theme.

On the suite side, VBench dissects video generation quality into specific, hierarchical and disentangled dimensions, each with tailored prompts and evaluation methods, motivated by the fact that existing metrics do not fully align with human perception and that an ideal system should inform future development [35]. VBench-2.0 shifts the target from superficial to intrinsic faithfulness — whether generated videos adhere to physical laws, commonsense reasoning, anatomical correctness and compositional integrity — evaluating five key dimensions (Human Fidelity, Creativity, Controllability, Physics, Commonsense) across five broad categories and 18 fine-grained capability dimensions, validating alignment with large-scale human preference labelling [36]. Compositional evaluation goes further: T2V-CompBench covers seven compositional categories with 1400 text prompts and argues that Inception Score, FID, FVD and CLIPScore cannot fully reflect compositionality, reporting that dynamic attribute binding is the most challenging category, that models struggle to distinguish "left" from "right" and can hardly understand moving directions, and that CLIP, ViCLIP, B-CLIP and B-BLEU show relatively low human correlations for fine-grained attributes, spatial relations and dynamics [37]. EvalCrafter similarly generates 700 prompts and evaluates with 17 objective metrics across visual, content, motion and text-video alignment qualities, criticising FVD as unsuitable for general text-to-video because it requires ground-truth videos, reporting that CLIP-Score has Spearman rho 6.3 versus BLIP-BLEU's 26.7 on alignment, and concluding that all current models are not satisfactory enough — even the best model it tested struggled with complex scenes, instruction following and entity details [38].

The metric-side negative results are sharper. FVD is "known to conflict with human perception occasionally"; it increases only slightly with large temporal corruption, temporal inconsistency on FaceForensics raises FVD by only about 3%, and on Sky Time-lapse one can reduce FVD by up to half by selecting candidate videos, with frozen motionless videos in the worst case matching or beating videos with motion — because I3D features from a supervised Kinetics-400 model are biased to the appearance of objects and backgrounds rather than motions, and self-supervised VideoMAE-v2 features cut that bias [21]. Correlations with human judgement confirm the diagnosis: for temporal transition completion, CLIP similarity correlates at -0.0879 and ViCLIP at 0.0599 against a human upper bound of 0.7011, while the benchmark's own TC-Score reaches 0.2977; TC-Bench also finds that all direct text-to-video models succeed in completing the described transition less than 10% of the time, image-to-video models achieve a much higher completion rate while text-to-video models reach only around 1%, and existing metrics assign higher scores to static scenes without compositional changes [22].

Capability benchmarks show how far absolute performance remains from the benchmark frontier. VideoPhy curates 688 high-quality, human-verified captions over solid-solid, solid-fluid and fluid-fluid interactions and generates videos from 12 text-to-video models; the best model generates videos adhering to the caption and physical laws for 39.6% of instances and the rest score below 20%, with Dream Machine showing a semantic adherence score of 61.9% but a physical commonsense score of only 21.8%, and the authors judging physical commonsense a harder task than judging semantic adherence [23]. The leaderboard view is harder still to interpret: a VBench leaderboard lists 69 benchmarked models and 19 measured metrics, with the top three rows carrying the caveat "Source: Self-reported", and the rank-1 model scoring 100% on human action but only 27.14% on appearance style, 29.03% on temporal style and 26.68% on overall consistency [39].

Human evaluation is the intended ground truth, and it is itself noisy. A survey of text-to-video human protocols standardises evaluation into four objective metrics (video quality, temporal quality, motion quality, text alignment) and two subjective ones (ethical robustness, human preference), finds that nearly 70% of prior work employs a comparison-based approach, and shows that untrained raters have very low agreement: Krippendorff's alpha for text alignment rises from 0.069 to 0.327 and for video quality from 0.185 to 0.411 once raters are trained, while scoring pairwise comparisons with the Rao and Kupper model reduces costs by approximately 50% — and even then a substantial disparity persists between open-source and closed-source models [40]. Read together with the metric correlations above, the evaluation literature suggests that model rankings at the top of today's leaderboards rest on automated scores whose alignment with human judgement is weakest exactly where video differs from images: motion, temporal ordering and physics [37][22][21].

## Trends and open problems
The clearest trend of the last two years is that the problem statement itself has changed from generating a good clip to running a long, interactive, multimodal service. NVIDIA's infrastructure analysis argues that once models move beyond short clips toward minute-long, high-fidelity video across variable durations, resolutions and aspect ratios, the next wave becomes multimodal, controllable, editable, audio-video synchronised and low-latency, and that long video generation "is not short video generation repeated over time" but an online process with memory, scheduling, synchronisation and error accumulation [26]. It documents the failure mode concretely — an early frame leaving a residual ghost in the last frame — and the hidden accounting controversy: VAE decoding, KV-cache movement, GPU synchronisation, CPU-GPU transfer and multi-shot scheduling can become large enough to change the system architecture, so users experience end-to-end latency rather than model-only FPS and reporting denoising speed alone becomes misleading for long videos [26]. Training-serving alignment adds a second failure channel, since quantisation errors can enter the generated history, be stored in the KV cache and condition future chunks [26]. The mitigations being explored include NVFP4 W4A4 inference and NVFP4 KV caching with global- and shot-level attention sinks [26], training-free sparse attention whose gains depend on fixing token grouping and interaction selection rather than on the sparsity level itself [33], and length extrapolation that stays within the base model's positional capacity [12].

The paradigm dispute between bidirectional diffusion and autoregressive generation is genuinely unresolved, and the sources disagree explicitly. One argument is architectural: full diffusion is "a great clip generator — and a poor streaming primitive", because it cannot emit the beginning of a video while the future is unresolved, extending duration increases the token set processed at every denoising step, and a prompt change mid-generation has no natural boundary; the proposed answer is AR diffusion that factorises time into frames or blocks so a clean block can be streamed and summarised into a KV cache while the next is denoised, citing a two-stage recipe that compresses a 50-step teacher into a four-step AR generator with KV caching [41]. The same source warns that the headline 60-second wall-clock comparison is not a controlled autoregressive-versus-bidirectional ablation because resolution, kernels and end-to-end accounting differ, and that AR does not imply few-step generation: exposure bias remains the core issue, since autoregressive generation conditions on its own past and a small error becomes context for the next block [41]. A more radical critique argues that today's stack satisfies neither condition of a native autoregressive video model — neither pretraining a next-frame conditional nor generating the next step in one forward pass — and that post-training cannot add either property retroactively; even at four denoising steps per block a full generation still costs N_blocks x K forward passes, block length remains an inference-time compromise (three frames in the stack considered), and short-clip appearance metrics say little about whether native autoregression works because bidirectional models may look better simply for having seen future frames during training [42]. The practical corollary, echoed in the efficiency literature, is that long-horizon evaluation is an unsolved problem: short-window metrics can miss identity drift, temporal inconsistency and control failures that appear minutes later, and one- and two-step models still trade motion richness, sharpness and diversity in ways current benchmarks only partially capture [41].

Physics and controllability are the least solved capability problems, and the evidence is first-principles rigorous. T2VPhysBench evaluates whether both open-source and commercial text-to-video systems obey twelve core physical laws — Newtonian mechanics, conservation principles and phenomenological effects — under a fully manual human evaluation over ten models, and finds that all models score below 0.60 on average in each law category, that the best performance on conservation laws peaks at 0.29 for Wan 2.1 while the worst is 0.13 for Dreamina and LTX Video, that law-specific prompt hints fail to remedy violations and can even produce negative impacts, and that models often break physical rules when explicitly instructed, with Kling dropping from 0.38 on phenomenon principles to 0.00 on counterfactual phenomenon prompts [24]. That counterfactual collapse suggests compliance rooted in memorised patterns rather than genuine physical reasoning [24], which is consistent with the suite-level finding that intrinsic faithfulness — physics, commonsense and compositionality — is precisely where fine-grained dimensions and human correlations are weakest [36][37][22]. Controllability is being attacked both behaviourally, through interactive motion controls distilled to real-time latency [10], and structurally, by enforcing temporal guarantees such as looping by construction instead of hoping the model learns them [34].

Safety, provenance and training data are being pushed by regulation rather than by technical consensus. The EU Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content supports AI Act Article 50 obligations, applicable from 2 August 2026, requiring providers to mark outputs of AI systems — audio, image, video, text — in a machine-readable format detectable as artificially generated or manipulated, with solutions that are effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as technically feasible, and requiring deployers to disclose deepfake content resembling existing persons or events; adherence to the code is voluntary but the transparency requirements themselves are legal obligations [27]. The training-data side is unsettled and litigation-driven: the U.S. Copyright Office notes that the fair-use question is the subject of intense debate with dozens of lawsuits pending, documents video-specific acquisition practice such as stream-ripping software used to download millions of video or subtitle files from YouTube, and records content-targeted curation for video models, while offering the licensed-data alternative where rights are cleared for commercial use [43]. Two further open threads run through the record: whether the compression bottleneck of latent video autoencoders will be resolved by better latents or by retraining the DiT around them [4], and whether unified any-to-any architectures will overtake specialised video pipelines, given that continuous-diffusion hybrids claim better scaling than discrete image tokens [18] while discrete-vocabulary models claim streaming and simplicity at some cost in image fidelity [13].
