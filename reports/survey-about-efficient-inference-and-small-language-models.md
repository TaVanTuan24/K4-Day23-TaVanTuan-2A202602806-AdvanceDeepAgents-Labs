# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: A Survey of Compression, Serving Systems, and Small-Model Recipes

## TL;DR
- Efficiency work splits into three levers that stack: quantize/prune the weights, compress the KV cache and schedule requests better, and train or distill a smaller model in the first place [1].
- Post-training quantization is close to free at 8-bit and acceptable at 4-bit, but 2-bit is a cliff rather than a slope: 4-bit is widely regarded as an optimal trade-off while reducing precision to 2-bit "usually triggers a catastrophic performance cliff" [2], and FP8 weight-and-activation quantization was reported lossless across all Llama-3.1 scales over 500,000 evaluations [3].
- Serving-side systems deliver the largest headline multipliers: paged KV-cache management up to 22x request rate over FasterTransformer [4], prefill/decode disaggregation 7.4x more requests at the same latency target [5], and 4-bit offloading running OPT-175B at 1 token/s on a single 16 GB GPU [6].
- Small models are now trained efficiency-first rather than shrunk afterwards: SmolLM2-1.7B on ~11T tokens for roughly $250,000 of compute [7], MiniCPM4-8B matching Qwen3-8B with 22% of its training data [8], and 3.8B Phi-4-Mini-Reasoning beating 7B/8B R1-distills on Math-500 [9].
- The field disagrees about the biggest remaining lever: one line of evidence reports an accuracy decline correlated with a steep rise in policy entropy as thinking is extended from 4,983 to 16,109 tokens on MATH-500 [10], another reports a 32B model plus a 72B reward model beating o1-preview on AIME-2024 [11], and agentic coding is found to burn 1000x-3500x more tokens than ordinary reasoning [12].

## Background
Efficient inference is the study of serving a language model with acceptable latency, throughput and memory under a fixed budget of accelerators. A 2024 survey attributes the problem to three structural causes - the large model size, the quadratic-complexity attention operation, and the auto-regressive decoding approach - and organizes remedies into data-level, model-level and system-level optimization [1]. That taxonomy is useful because the individual techniques rarely work in isolation: the same survey notes that weight-only quantization targets the memory-bound decoding stage while weight-activation quantization targets the compute-bound prefill stage, so the right choice depends on which phase dominates [1].

The reason compression is so effective is the uneven cost profile of a transformer. In models at or beyond 6.7B parameters, feed-forward and attention projection layers account for 95% of the consumed parameters and 65-85% of the computation, which is why methods that attack only those layers can cut memory in half while retaining full-precision performance [13]. Model-level efficiency has a long lineage: sequence-level knowledge distillation for machine translation produced a student that ran 10 times faster than its state-of-the-art teacher with little loss in performance, and applying weight pruning on top yielded a student with 13x fewer parameters at a 0.4 BLEU decrease [14]. Small language models inherit that lineage but are now usually trained directly rather than distilled from a giant teacher [15].

Definitions matter for the small-model half of this survey. One measurement survey restricts its scope to transformer-based decoder-only models between 100M and 5B parameters and analyzes 59 open-source models across architectures, training datasets and training algorithms, benchmarking on-device latency and memory footprint as well as task accuracy [15]. A more generous recent definition treats small language models as 1-12B parameters, sometimes up to 20B, and argues they are sufficient and often superior for agentic workloads where the objective is schema- and API-constrained accuracy rather than open-ended generation [16]. A position paper draws the line at what fits a consumer device with practically low latency, judging that most models below 10 billion parameters counted as small in 2025 [17].

## Quantization: near-lossless at 8-bit, viable at 4-bit, a cliff at 2-bit
Weight-only quantization is the most widely deployed efficiency technique, and the foundational methods converge on 3-4 bit weights with small reported losses. GPTQ quantized the 175B-parameter GPT family in approximately four GPU hours to 3 or 4 bits per weight with negligible accuracy degradation, reporting speedups over FP16 of about 3.25x on an A100 and 4.5x on an A6000, and claimed to be the first method to execute a 175B model inside a single GPU for generative inference [18]. AWQ reached the same regime from a different premise, finding that not all weights matter equally and that protecting only 1% salient weights - selected from the activation distribution rather than the weight distribution - greatly reduces quantization error, with no backpropagation or reconstruction required [19]. Its TinyChat runtime reported more than 3x speedup over the HuggingFace FP16 implementation on both desktop and mobile GPUs, which is how the authors framed deploying 70B Llama-2 on mobile GPUs [19].

The alternative axis is quantizing activations as well as weights, which buys throughput in the compute-bound prefill phase rather than memory savings in decoding. SmoothQuant is training-free and enables 8-bit weight and 8-bit activation (W8A8) quantization by offline-migrating the quantization difficulty from activations to weights through a mathematically equivalent transformation, reporting up to 1.56x speedup and 2x memory reduction with negligible accuracy loss and enabling a 530B model to be served within a single node [20]. LLM.int8() attacks the same problem with vector-wise quantization plus a mixed-precision decomposition that isolates emergent outlier feature dimensions into 16-bit while more than 99.9% of values are still multiplied in 8-bit, cutting inference memory by half while retaining full-precision performance [13]. A secondary compression survey independently restates the GPTQ figures - roughly four GPU hours, 3-4 bits, approximately 3.25x A100 acceleration - and characterizes AWQ as retaining the top 1% of parameters at full precision for nearly 8x compression [21].

The contested question is how far down the bit-width scale this reasoning survives. A 2024 study of FP8, INT8 and INT4 across the entire Llama-3.1 family spanning over 500,000 individual evaluations found FP8 weight and activation quantization lossless across all model scales, properly tuned W8A8-INT incurring a surprisingly low 1-3% accuracy degradation, and INT4 weight-only quantization competitive with 8-bit integer weight-and-activation quantization [3]. A separate evaluation of quantized instruction-tuned models from 7B up to 405B found that quantizing a larger LLM down to the size of a smaller FP16 model generally performs better across most benchmarks - except for hallucination detection and instruction following - and that weight-only methods often do better in larger models [22]. The same study warned that MT-Bench has limited discriminatory power among recent high-performing models, which matters because much of the quantization evidence in circulation rests on that kind of aggregate score [22]. A 405B-scale companion result is that task difficulty does not significantly affect the accuracy degradation caused by quantization, so hard benchmarks are not automatically the sensitive ones [22].

At the low end the evidence is consistently negative. A 2024 study of ten post-training quantization and LoRA-finetuning methods on LLaMA3 from 1 to 8 bits found that LLaMA3 still suffers non-negligible degradation, especially at ultra-low bit-width, and highlighted the significant performance gap that needs to be bridged [23]. The sharpest characterization comes from a study of quantization failure modes that distinguishes two regimes: while 4-bit quantization is widely regarded as an optimal trade-off, reducing precision to 2-bit with common methods such as GPTQ "usually triggers a catastrophic performance cliff", particularly in tasks requiring precise factual knowledge; the study reports that at 4-bit the correct answer suffers an answer-rank drop but typically stays within the Top-5, whereas at 2-bit the rank falls to thousands, close to random guessing [2]. The same study measured how strongly the quantization error aligns with the signal subspace - similarity of about 0.8 at 2-bit against roughly 0.3 at 4-bit - and found the 2-bit damage resistant to repair: training-free repair mitigated the 4-bit mode but remained ineffective for what the authors call computation collapse [2]. Independent k-quantization experiments across eight models on MMLU-Pro, CRUXEval and MuSR reached the same conclusion from the other direction: one model fell from 55.32% on MMLU-Pro at Q8_0 to 38.83% at Q2_K, Phi-3-Mini's perplexity rose from 6.41 to an unusually high 195.79 at Q2_K, and the authors concluded there is no universally optimal quantization level, with moderate bit precisions such as Q3_K-Q6_K offering a practical compromise [24]. A further caution is that numerical metrics under-report the damage: an analysis of GPTQ-quantized LLaMA-2-7B found systematic smoothness degradation beyond numerical precision loss, with a rapid reduction of effective token candidates in the prediction neighborhood leading to a sparser decoding tree and degraded generation quality [25].

Deployment-level comparisons are more optimistic than the accuracy studies, partly because kernel quality differs enormously between formats. One practitioner comparison reports that 4-bit quantization cuts memory by 75% and that on a Qwen2.5-32B model served through vLLM, AWQ with Marlin kernels reached 741 tokens per second, GPTQ with Marlin 712, FP16 461, bitsandbytes 168 and GGUF 93, noting that optimized kernels can beat unquantized FP16 because the memory-bandwidth savings exceed dequantization overhead [26]. The same source estimates 4-bit quality retention of roughly 95% for AWQ, 95% or above for bitsandbytes, about 92% for GGUF Q4_K_M and near 90% for GPTQ on general text tasks [26]. These aggregate figures are consistent with the lossless-at-8-bit, mild-loss-at-4-bit picture, but they also illustrate why single-number comparisons should be treated carefully across different harnesses.

## Pruning, sparsity and distillation: cheaper than quantization, but costlier in accuracy
Pruning claims the same memory savings as quantization with no arithmetic reformatting, and the flagship results are striking. SparseGPT pruned GPT-family models to at least 50% sparsity in one-shot with no retraining and minimal accuracy loss, processed OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B in under 4.5 hours, reached 60% unstructured sparsity with negligible perplexity increase, and reported that more than 100 billion weights could be ignored at inference time [27]. Wanda requires no retraining or weight update and prunes weights by the smallest magnitude multiplied by the corresponding input activations on a per-output basis, motivated by emergent large-magnitude features in LLMs; it significantly outperforms magnitude pruning and is competitive with methods that use intensive weight updates [28].

The accuracy-ledger for pruning, however, is more sobering than for quantization, and the trade-off depends strongly on sparsity structure. The Wanda authors state that with no fine-tuning there is a noticeable gap between sparse pruned LLMs and original dense LLMs, though the gap diminishes as model size grows; for unstructured sparsity, 50% sparse LLaMA-65B reached 66.67% zero-shot accuracy against 65.38% for dense LLaMA-30B and 50% sparse LLaMA-2-13B reached 60.83% against 59.71% for dense LLaMA-7B [29]. For structured sparsity the trend reversed and large sparse LLMs had worse zero-shot performance than small dense ones without fine-tuning [29]. The absolute costs are visible on one model: LLaMA-7B scored 59.99% dense versus 54.21% at 50% Wanda sparsity, 52.76% at 4:8 and 48.53% at 2:4, with WikiText perplexity rising from 5.68 to 7.26 and 11.53 respectively, while magnitude pruning at 50% reached 17.29 [29]. Fine-tuning narrows but does not close the gap, with LoRA lifting structured 2:4 sparse LLaMA-7B to 54.46% and full-parameter fine-tuning lifting the 50% sparse model to 58.15% against the 59.99% dense baseline [29]. The conclusion is that pruning needs a recovery step where quantization mostly does not.

Sparse attention is the third sparsity frontier and the least settled. The largest empirical analysis to date compared six training-free sparse attention methods across model families, sequences up to 128K tokens and sparsity levels up to 0.95 on nine tasks, and found no clear strategy that performs best across tasks and phases; even moderate sparsity levels often cause significant performance degradation on at least one task, so sparse attention is not a universal solution [30]. Two nuances from that study are more useful than the headline: larger sparse models outperform smaller dense ones at equivalent cost, improving the Pareto frontier under an isoFLOPS analysis, and the level of sparsity that can statistically guarantee accuracy preservation is higher during decoding than during prefilling and correlates with model size [30]. It also reports a practical blocker, that fine-grained per-query importance estimation during prefilling remains impractical because of both estimation cost and the lack of sparse kernels that translate fine-grained sparsity into wall-clock gains [30].

Distillation closes the loop between the compression and small-model literatures, because it is the mechanism by which large-model capability is transferred into a model that is cheap to serve. The classical result remains the reference point: sequence-level distillation eliminated the need for beam search even on the original teacher model, and the best student ran 10 times faster with little loss in performance and 4.2/1.7 BLEU better than a baseline trained without distillation [14]. Both of the small flagship families released in 2025 use this lever: Gemma 3 models are trained with distillation and are reported to outperform Gemma 2 for both pre-trained and instruction-finetuned versions [31], and Qwen3 leverages knowledge from flagship models to reduce the computational resources required to build smaller-scale models [32].

## Serving systems: KV cache, paging, phase splitting and the edge
The systems literature attacks a narrower bottleneck than compression does: the memory traffic and scheduling waste of the KV cache during decoding, plus the interference between prefill and decode. FlashAttention made the memory hierarchy explicit with an IO-aware exact attention that tiles computation to reduce reads and writes between GPU HBM and on-chip SRAM, reporting a 15% end-to-end wall-clock speedup on BERT-large at sequence length 512, 3x speedup on GPT-2 at 1K and 2.4x on long-range arena, and enabling Path-X at 61.4% accuracy at 16K sequence length [33]. The follow-up diagnosed the first version as memory-efficient but reaching only 25-40% of theoretical maximum FLOPs/s, and fixed parallelism and work partitioning to reach 50-73% of theoretical maximum FLOPs/s on A100 with about 2x speedup over FlashAttention and up to 225 TFLOPs/s per A100 (72% model FLOPs utilization) in end-to-end GPT-style training [34]. This progression matters for the survey's verdict: kernel-level work is real but incremental next to the scheduling and cache-management gains below.

Speculative decoding attacks the serial dependency of generation rather than memory bandwidth, and it is distinctive for being exact. The original formulation computes several tokens in parallel by running a smaller approximation model as a draft and verifying its candidates with the target model in parallel, speeding up existing off-the-shelf models without retraining or architecture changes and reporting a 2x-3x acceleration on T5-XXL with identical outputs [35]. Speculative sampling reached the same conclusion with a modified rejection sampling scheme that preserves the distribution of the target model within hardware numerics, achieving a 2-2.5x decoding speedup with Chinchilla, a 70B model, in a distributed setup without compromising sample quality or modifying the model itself [36]. The technique's trade-off is opposite to quantization's: it costs extra compute and memory for the draft model but carries no accuracy risk by construction, which is why it composes well with the lossy methods above rather than replacing them.

The largest single multipliers come from managing the KV cache as memory rather than as a tensor. PagedAttention and the vLLM system improved throughput of popular LLMs by 2-4x at the same latency compared with FasterTransformer and Orca; on ShareGPT, vLLM sustained 1.7x-2.7x higher request rates than Orca (Oracle) and 2.7x-8x compared with Orca (Max), and up to 22x higher request rates than FasterTransformer, with memory sharing improving beam search gains from 1.3x in basic sampling to 2.3x at beam width 6 and saving 37.6%-55.2% of memory in beam search [4]. Notably the PagedAttention kernel itself added 20-26% higher attention kernel latency than FasterTransformer's kernel and the system still won end-to-end, illustrating that throughput in these systems is dominated by batching efficiency rather than raw kernel speed [4]. The vLLM team's own blog reports the same effect against a weaker baseline, up to 24x higher throughput than HuggingFace Transformers on LLaMA-7B over an A10G and LLaMA-13B over an A100, with PagedAttention memory waste under 4% and memory sharing cutting usage by up to 55% for up to 2.2x throughput improvement [37].

Phase splitting targets a different inefficiency: prompt computation is compute-intensive while token generation is memory-intensive, so the two phases want different hardware and parallelism. DistServe assigns prefill and decoding to different GPUs to eliminate prefill-decoding interference and co-optimizes resource allocation and parallelism per phase, reporting it can serve 7.4x more requests or a 12.6x tighter SLO than state-of-the-art systems while staying within latency constraints for more than 90% of requests [5]. Splitwise splits the same two phases across machines and reports up to 1.4x higher throughput at 20% lower cost, or alternatively 2.35x more throughput under the same power and cost budgets [38]. Offloading takes the opposite approach and trades throughput for feasibility: FlexGen aggregates GPU, CPU and disk memory and compute with 4-bit compression of weights and attention cache, running OPT-175B on a single 16 GB GPU at a generation throughput of 1 token/s with an effective batch size of 144, and a 30B model on a 16 GB GPU across 7 HELM sub-scenarios in 21 hours [6]. Together these results define a spectrum from latency-critical datacenter serving to memory-constrained single-device deployment rather than a single efficiency ranking.

KV-cache eviction is the compression technique that lives inside the serving stack. H2O dynamically retains a balance of recent and heavy-hitter tokens based on the observation that a small subset of tokens carries most of the attention value, and reports up to 29x throughput improvement over DeepSpeed Zero-Inference and HuggingFace Accelerate on OPT-6.7B and OPT-30B at 20% heavy hitters, up to 1.9x latency reduction at the same batch size, and up to 5x KV-cache memory reduction without accuracy degradation [39]. StreamingLLM takes a complementary finding - that strong attention scores flow to the initial tokens as an attention sink even when those tokens are not semantically important - and keeps the KV of those initial tokens so that models trained with a finite attention window generalize to effectively infinite sequence length without fine-tuning, working up to 4 million tokens and outperforming the sliding-window recomputation baseline by up to 22.2x [40]. A 2025 system combines static and dynamic sparsity by converting half of the attention heads into nearly free streaming heads, reporting that only a constant number of KV pages is required to preserve long-context capability irrespective of context length, with up to 2.9x faster prefilling and 1.3-2.1x faster decoding than vLLM while maintaining long-context accuracy [41].

On-device deployment is where these techniques are forced to coexist under the tightest budget. Transformer-Lite combines dynamic-shape inference, operator-level optimizations, FP4 quantization and sub-tensor KV-cache handling on Snapdragon 8 Gen 3 and Dimensity 9300 phones, reaching 330 token/s prefill and 30 token/s decode for Gemma 2B, over 10x prefill speedup and 2-3x decode speedup versus CPU-based FastLLM and GPU-based MLC-LLM, and deploying Qwen1.5 14B at 54 token/s prefill and 5 token/s decode on a 24 GB phone [42]. Apple's Core ML work reports Int4 quantization plus a stateful KV cache giving Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct about 33 tokens/s decode on an M1 Max, roughly twice the speed of the Float16 model, while shrinking the model from 16 GB to 4.2 GB (about a 4x reduction) and reaching a TTFT of 51.91 ms in the fully optimized configuration [43]. The reported scaling is informative: extend throughput fell from 39.44 tokens/s at context 128 to 21.46 at context 8192, showing that context length, not parameter count alone, sets the mobile ceiling [43].

## Small language models: data-centric training and reasoning transfer
The 2024-2025 small-model wave is defined by training efficiency rather than by architecture. SmolLM2's 1.7B model was overtrained on about 11 trillion tokens using a multi-stage mixture of web text with specialized math, code and instruction-following data plus new datasets FineMath, Stack-Edu and SmolTalk, trained on 256 H100s with a warmup-stable-decay schedule and a peak learning rate of 5.0x10^-4 for roughly $250,000 of GPU compute, and claimed to outperform Qwen2.5-1.5B and Llama3.2-1B [7]. The associated model card documents the family at 135M, 360M and 1.7B parameters with the 1.7B variant trained on 11 trillion tokens from FineWeb-Edu, DCLM and The Stack, and the instruct version built through supervised fine-tuning followed by DPO with UltraFeedback [44]. MiniCPM4 pushes the same argument further toward devices with 0.5B and 8B versions and states that its cleaned data enables satisfactory performance from just 8 trillion training tokens, that MiniCPM4-8B is comparable to Qwen3-8B using 22% of its training data, and that it achieves a 7-fold speed improvement on 128K-length documents on end-side devices, using trainable sparse attention, a ternary model variant and a dedicated inference system [8]. The two families therefore differ in emphasis - SmolLM2 optimizes data quality per token at fixed scale, MiniCPM4 optimizes tokens per device-second - but agree that data curation, not parameter count, is the lever.

Post-training is where small models are pushed toward reasoning. SmolTulu adapts the Tulu 3 post-training pipeline onto SmolLM2-1.7B and reports state-of-the-art sub-2B instruction following at 67.7% on IFEval (+11 points) and 51.6% on GSM8K (+3.4 points), with an alternate version at 57.1% on ARC (+5.4 points); its ablation found that reasoning tasks such as ARC and GSM8K benefit from higher learning-rate-to-batch-size ratios while pattern-recognition tasks such as HellaSwag and IFEval prefer lower ratios [45]. Phi-4-Mini-Reasoning shows how far this can be pushed at 3.8B parameters with a four-step recipe of large-scale mid-training on distilled long chain-of-thought data, supervised fine-tuning on high-quality long CoT, rollout DPO on a curated preference set, and reinforcement learning with verifiable reward, after which it outperformed DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B by 3.2 points and R1-Distill-Llama-8B by 7.7 points on Math-500 [9]. The family releases confirm the direction at both ends of the scale: Gemma 3 spans 1 to 27B parameters with vision, at least 128K context and a KV-cache reduction obtained by increasing the ratio of local to global attention layers, and its post-training recipe makes Gemma3-4B-IT competitive with Gemma2-27B-IT and Gemma3-27B-IT comparable to Gemini-1.5-Pro [31]; Qwen3 covers 0.6 to 235B parameters across dense and mixture-of-experts architectures with a unified thinking/non-thinking framework and a thinking budget that allocates compute adaptively at inference, expanding multilingual support from 29 to 119 languages under Apache 2.0 [32].

The counterweight is that long chain-of-thought data can actively damage small models. A 2025 study identifies long CoT degradation: SLMs at or below 3B parameters trained on limited long CoT data experience significant performance deterioration across the Qwen2.5, LLaMA3 and Gemma3 families, and in some settings models trained on only 8k long CoT examples lost up to 75% of their original performance before fine-tuning [46]. For some particularly small models even 220k long CoT examples failed to recover or surpass pre-fine-tuning performance, and the authors attribute the effect to error accumulation, noting it also harms downstream reinforcement learning but can be alleviated by sufficiently scaled supervised fine-tuning [46]. Combined with the earlier result that post-training ratios that help reasoning tasks hurt pattern-recognition tasks [45], the picture is that small-model post-training is a genuinely narrow trade-off rather than a monotone improvement.

## Benchmarks, negative results and where efficiency techniques break
Efficiency evaluation has moved toward explicit latency contracts rather than aggregate scores. MLPerf Inference 5.1 replaced GPT-J with Llama3.1-8B as its small-LLM benchmark, uses vLLM as the reference implementation, separates the prompt phase measured as time to first token from the generation phase measured as time per output token with throughput in tokens/s, and sets server-scenario thresholds of TTFT at or below 2 seconds and TPOT at or below 100 milliseconds [47]. It added an interactive scenario with TTFT at or below 0.5 seconds and TPOT at or below 30 milliseconds, equivalent to roughly 1,600 words per minute versus about 480 for the server scenario, and requires edge submissions to report single-stream latency at the 90th percentile because per-token latencies do not capture system performance for a given sequence [47]. The benchmark's context length also jumped from 2,048 tokens for GPT-J to 128,000 for Llama3.1-8B, which is itself evidence that the cost center moved to long context [47].

Accuracy-side evidence is where the negative results concentrate, and it is consistent enough to be treated as a finding rather than an anecdote. The two-regime quantization picture - gradual loss that stays usable to 4-bit, and a catastrophic performance cliff at 2-bit where the correct answer's rank falls to thousands and the quantization error becomes aligned with the signal subspace at around 0.8 similarity versus roughly 0.3 at 4-bit [2] - agrees with independent k-quantization measurements showing MMLU-Pro falling from 55.32% to 38.83% and Phi-3-Mini perplexity rising from 6.41 to 195.79 at Q2_K [24], with the LLaMA3 study's finding of non-negligible degradation especially at ultra-low bit-width [23], and with the observation that the damage extends beyond numerical fit into smoothness and decoding-tree diversity [25]. The same failure-mode study reports that the answer rank degrades before accuracy does, and that training-free repair works at 4-bit but not at 2-bit [2]. The practical resolution offered across these papers is not a single optimal bit-width but a range: moderate precisions such as Q3_K-Q6_K are described as a practical compromise [24], while W8A8-FP was reported lossless and W8A8-INT only 1-3% down [3]. Perplexity is explicitly flagged as an insufficient standalone predictor of downstream performance [24].

Task-level exceptions and structural effects cut across the bit-width story. Quantizing a larger model to the size of a smaller FP16 one generally wins across most benchmarks, but the exceptions are hallucination detection and instruction following, which means the tasks most relevant to deployed assistant behaviour are precisely where the equivalence breaks [22]. Pruning is worse than quantization on the same accuracy budget and reverses its scaling pattern for structured sparsity, since unstructured sparsity lets large sparse models beat small dense ones while structured sparsity does not without fine-tuning [29]. Sparse attention in turn has no dominant method, and even moderate sparsity degrades at least one of nine tasks, with the guarantee of accuracy preservation being stronger in decoding than in prefilling [30]. On the small-model side, the recurring failure mode is not raw knowledge but tool behaviour: a 2026 study of search agents found that despite possessing less parametric knowledge, small models below 4B parameters invoke search tools less frequently and are more prone to hallucination, identifying parametric hallucination as a central failure mode, and reported that an always-search policy improved scores by 17.3 on Bamboogle and 15.3 on HotpotQA while adaptive search strategies in small models often degraded performance [48]. An on-premises deployment guide states the same limitation in deployment terms, noting that small models are limited in scope and accuracy compared with LLMs and that retrieval augmentation and fine-tuning are needed to compensate, and warns that models with larger context sizes above 50k consume more RAM and that, for Phi-3, context should be reduced to 8k or 16k, where a larger context can itself impact accuracy [49].

## Competing directions: test-time compute versus small models, agents, and mixtures of experts
The clearest disagreement in the field is whether to spend compute at inference to make a small model better or to make the model smaller and cheaper in the first place. The optimistic case reports that a particle-filtering inference-time scaling method let even a 7B model match the performance of the significantly larger reasoning model o1-preview on Math500, and that on AIME-2024 a 32B Qwen-instruct model paired with a 72B process reward model outperformed o1-preview by one question without any distillation from R1, while noting that the amortization of test-time scaling by training the transition model itself as a reward model remains an untested hypothesis [11]. The skeptical case reports a consistent pattern of initial improvement followed by decline due to overthinking, with a steep rise in policy entropy as thinking is extended from 4,983 to 16,109 tokens on MATH-500, and GSM-8K entropy rising 12x from 0.23 to 2.79, concluding that observed gains from more thinking are not true indicators of improved reasoning; it proposes parallel thinking with majority vote instead, reporting up to 20% higher accuracy than extended thinking and up to 22% higher under a 16K token budget than sequential scaling, while acknowledging that the study was limited to mid-sized models for resource reasons [10]. The two positions are not strictly contradictory - one optimizes a search procedure, the other measures naive token extension - but they recommend opposite default policies, and the scope limitations on both sides are explicit.

Agentic workloads sharpen the dispute because their token economics are an order of magnitude worse than ordinary inference. An analysis of trajectories from eight frontier models on SWE-bench Verified found agentic coding uniquely expensive, consuming 1000x more tokens than code reasoning and code chat with input tokens rather than output tokens driving the cost, quantified in the body as 3,500x a typical single-round reasoning task and 1,200x a multi-round chatting task [12]. The same study found token usage highly variable and inherently stochastic, with runs on the same task differing by up to 30x in total tokens and with higher usage not translating into higher accuracy because accuracy often peaks at intermediate cost and saturates at higher cost; it also found frontier models fail to predict their own token usage, with weak-to-moderate correlations up to 0.39 and systematic underestimation, calling reliable self-prediction an open problem [12]. Against that backdrop the case for small models in agentic systems rests on cost: a position paper estimates that serving a 7B small model is 10-30x cheaper in latency, energy consumption and FLOPs than a 70-175B LLM, treats the barriers to small-model agents as practical rather than fundamental, and explicitly declines to give a timeline while noting the large capital bets already made on centralized LLM inference [17]. A survey of small models for agentic systems goes further and argues that guided decoding, strict JSON Schema outputs and validator-first tool execution close much of the capability gap, often letting small models match or surpass larger ones on tool use, function calling and retrieval at 10x-100x lower token cost, while delineating limits where fallback to a larger model remains valuable in open-domain reasoning and some long-horizon planning [16].

Multi-agent architectures are the third contested direction, and the disagreement is explicitly about accounting. A 2026 study finds that single-agent systems consistently match or outperform multi-agent systems on multi-hop reasoning when reasoning tokens are held constant, argues from a data processing inequality perspective that under a fixed reasoning-token budget and perfect context utilization single-agent systems are more information-efficient, and claims many reported multi-agent advantages are better explained by unaccounted computation and context effects than by architectural benefits, identifying significant artifacts in API-based budget control in Gemini 2.5 [50]. It compares against five multi-agent architectures over Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama and Gemini 2.5 on FRAMES and MuSiQue, and gives its own boundary condition honestly: under context substitution the single-agent system is ahead at alpha=0.3, the two converge at alpha=0.5, and the sequential multi-agent configuration is clearly better at alpha=0.7 [50]. The practical implication for efficient inference is that the accounting unit matters as much as the architecture: if multi-agent gains vanish once tokens are equalized, then efficiency evaluations that ignore total tokens will systematically overrate agentic designs.

Mixture-of-experts and long-context sparsity are the directions where efficiency research is currently most systems-focused. MoE scaling is undermined by load imbalance: under expert parallelism the most burdened expert dictates the overall delay, a phenomenon defined as the straggler effect, and capacity-aware token dropping plus rerouting overflowed tokens to underutilized experts yielded a 0.2% average performance increase and a 1.94x inference speedup on Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct [51]. Qwen3's flagship family already ships dense and MoE variants across 0.6 to 235B parameters with an inference-time thinking budget, which places compute allocation for both sparse activation and reasoning within the same architectural decision [32]. Long-context serving is converging on hybrid static and dynamic sparsity, where half the attention heads become nearly free streaming heads and only a constant number of KV pages is needed irrespective of context length, giving up to 2.9x faster prefilling and 1.3-2.1x faster decoding than vLLM [41]; this is consistent with the sparse-attention finding that decoding tolerates far more sparsity than prefilling [30], and with the KV-eviction results that first showed that only a small subset of tokens carries most of the attention value [39] and that preserving attention-sink tokens allows effectively unbounded sequence length [40].

## Trends and open problems
The last two years moved the field from compressing existing large models to training small ones deliberately, and from single-technique papers to benchmarks with explicit latency contracts. Model-level compression results such as 3-4 bit weight quantization in four GPU hours [18] and one-shot 50-60% pruning [27] are now preconditions rather than contributions; the novel work is in recipes, where SmolLM2 shows that 11 trillion tokens and roughly $250,000 of compute can produce a competitive 1.7B model [7], and MiniCPM4 shows that 8 trillion well-curated tokens can make an 8B model comparable to Qwen3-8B while being 7x faster on 128K documents on end devices [8]. On the systems side, the measurement standard has shifted as well: MLPerf Inference 5.1 replaced a 6B model with Llama3.1-8B at 128,000 tokens of context and added an interactive scenario with 0.5-second TTFT and 30-millisecond TPOT thresholds, which makes interactive long-context serving the reference workload rather than offline batched generation [47].

Several questions remain genuinely unresolved. First, the safe operating point of quantization is not settled: FP8 is reported lossless and W8A8-INT only 1-3% down over more than 500,000 evaluations [3], yet the same bit-widths are reported to degrade hallucination detection and instruction following specifically [22] and to hurt LLaMA3 at ultra-low bit-width [23], with the 2-bit regime remaining resistant to training-free repair [2] and numerical metrics failing to capture the loss of smoothness and decoding diversity [25]. Second, whether inference-time compute or smaller models is the cheaper route to capability is disputed with concrete and mutually inconsistent numbers, from an accuracy decline correlated with rising policy entropy under extended thinking [10] to a 32B model plus a 72B reward model beating o1-preview on AIME-2024 [11], and the resolution may depend on whether compute is spent on search rather than on token extension. Third, agentic accounting is an open methodological problem: token consumption varies up to 30x on identical tasks, accuracy saturates rather than improving with cost, and models cannot predict their own token usage with correlations only up to 0.39 [12], while single-agent systems are found to match multi-agent ones at equal budgets [50] and small models are argued to be sufficient for constrained agentic tasks at 10-100x lower token cost [16]. Fourth, small-model capability limits are still being mapped: long CoT training can destroy up to 75% of a sub-3B model's prior performance [46], small search agents hallucinate instead of calling tools and degrade under adaptive search policies [48], and on-premises guidance still recommends shrinking context for models such as Phi-3 because large contexts increase RAM use and can reduce accuracy [49]. Fifth, sparse attention has no dominant method, degrades at least one of nine tasks even at moderate sparsity, and lacks the kernels to convert fine-grained prefilling sparsity into wall-clock gain [30], while MoE efficiency depends on taming load imbalance that a 1.94x speedup on Mixtral-8x7B only partly addresses [51]. The unifying theme is that efficiency gains are now well quantified for throughput and memory but weakly quantified for the behaviours - instruction following, hallucination detection, tool use and long-horizon planning - that determine whether a cheap model can actually be deployed.
